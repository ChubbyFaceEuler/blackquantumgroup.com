Anatomy of a look-ahead artifact: the backtest that was three-quarters imaginary

A +92 bps/week walk-forward result that fell to an insignificant +23 once two separate leaks were removed — and ended up losing to the simpler baseline it was supposed to beat. Published July 2026.

This is the second post-mortem in our series on bugs that passed our checks (the first, on a corrupted order-book reconstruction, is here). It is about look-ahead in its two most seductive forms: neither was a mislabeled timestamp, and neither would be caught by asking "does any feature use tomorrow's price?" Both were architectural — and they stacked.

The setup

We were testing an architecture idea on CME energy futures: augment a per-bar prediction model with a daily regime feature — a low-dimensional summary of what kind of day the market is having, learned from a large panel of technical and cross-sectional factors. Call the simple baseline v4 and the regime-augmented model v6. The harness looked respectable: 79 weeks of rolling walk-forward, retraining every 4 weeks, intraday horizon, transaction costs included.

The number that was too good

v6 came back at +91.55 bps/week net, 84.8% positive weeks, against a baseline around +33. The regime feature appeared to nearly triple the edge. Every box a skeptic would ask about — walk-forward, out-of-sample, net of costs — was checked. This is the moment that decides whether you have a research process or a hype process.

Leak one: the features that were constants

Most of the feature panel was generated by engines that return a full time series. A few engines, though, exposed only a "give me all features" method that returned the final bar's scalar value. Assigning a scalar to a DataFrame column broadcasts it to every row. Result: 218 of 274 features were per-symbol constants — each one computed over the full three-year history and then pasted onto every row, including rows three years before the value was knowable.

Why didn't they get filtered out? The harness dropped zero-variance columns — but it computed variance pooled across symbols, and the constants differ by symbol. So they survived, functioning as look-ahead-contaminated fixed effects feeding a cross-sectional strategy. The tell, once we looked: nunique() == 1 per symbol, across every one of those columns. One line of diagnostics, never run.

Leak two: the regime that knew tomorrow

The regime for day t was computed from all of day t's cross-section — including bars that hadn't happened yet — then attached to every bar of day t. A model predicting at 10am knew the statistical character of the whole day, including the afternoon. No individual timestamp was wrong. The leak lived in the aggregation boundary: a per-day summary applied intraday is future information for every bar before the close.

The decomposition

We removed the leaks one at a time, changing nothing else, on the same 79-week harness:

ConfigurationNet bps/weekAssessment
Original v6 (both leaks)+91.5584.8% positive weeks
Constant features removed+73.98leak one ≈ 18 bps
Regime made strictly causal too+23.02t ≈ 1.3 — not significant
Simple v4 baseline, same window+32.90NW t ≈ 2.3, 72% positive weeks

Read the last two rows twice. Honestly measured, the sophisticated architecture is not significant — and it is worse than the simple baseline it had appeared to triple. Roughly three-quarters of the headline number was artifact, and the bigger contribution (~51 bps of it) was the regime leak — the one that a feature-level leakage scan can never see.

One day of foresight — not tomorrow's prices, just a summary of today's own character — was worth ~51 bps/week of imaginary edge. Leakage doesn't need to be dramatic to be fatal; it needs only to be systematic.

What tipped us off

Three things, none of them a failing unit test:

An implausibly strong statistic. The contaminated run showed a rank information coefficient of +0.18 on minute bars. Anyone who has worked with intraday data knows that number is from another planet (the honest run's +0.05 is a believable one). Calibrated priors about effect sizes are a leakage detector.

Cross-context inconsistency. The same architecture was a null result on a different market's data but a miracle on this one. When an effect size varies wildly across contexts, suspect the measurement before the world.

A mechanical causality question. The regime leak fell to one question asked input-by-input: "at the moment this bar's prediction is made, is this value already known?" The per-day aggregate failed it instantly.

Pre-registration, so we couldn't move the goalposts

Before rerunning anything we wrote down the fix and the verdict criteria — what would count as the architecture winning, losing, or inconclusive — before seeing the results. From the internal retest protocol, verbatim:

"Report the verdict honestly whichever way it falls. The point of this handoff is to be as skeptical of 'v6 is dead' as of 'v6 is +44 bps.'"

A pre-registered fallback hypothesis — maybe the regime is useful as a risk overlay rather than as alpha — also failed its causal test: gating exposure by regime reduced out-of-sample Sharpe from 1.70 to 1.05. Both verdicts were accepted as written. The +23 was not spun into a win, and the architecture did not ship.

The uncomfortable epilogue

This was not our first over-claim on this line of research. Our internal notes record four earlier claims that were retracted after null tests or ablations — including a feature-selection method whose "significant" edge turned out to match random selection exactly. Every retraction came from our own testing rather than external correction, which is the system working; that it took several cycles is the part we publish so we remember it.

Lessons

1. Interrogate aggregation boundaries. Look-ahead hides in summaries: daily statistics applied intraday, full-window normalizations, regime labels, cluster assignments. Ask of every input: known at prediction time, yes or no?

2. Run the dumb diagnostics. nunique() per symbol would have exposed 218 broken features in one line. Variance filters, like range checks, confirm data is well-formed — not that it is correct, and not that it is causal.

3. Calibrate priors on effect sizes. A rank IC of +0.18 on minute bars should trigger an investigation, not a celebration. The best leakage detector we own is the sentence "that's too good."

4. Pre-register the retest. Writing win/lose/inconclusive criteria before seeing results is cheap, and it is the only thing that reliably stops a +23-with-SE-18 from being narrated into a victory.

5. The simple baseline is the real benchmark. The honest comparison wasn't "is the number big" but "does the added machinery beat v4." It didn't. Complexity has to pay rent under causal evaluation, or it goes.
