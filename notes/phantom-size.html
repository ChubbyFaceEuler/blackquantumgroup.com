Anatomy of a subtle backtest bug: the phantom size

How one ignored field in an order-book reconstruction corrupted every size-based feature we had — while passing every check we thought to run. Published July 2026.

Everyone who builds trading research infrastructure has a story like this one. Almost nobody publishes theirs. We think that's backwards: the bug is the least proprietary thing a quant lab owns, and the discovery method is the most useful thing it can share.

The setup

We reconstruct limit order books for CME futures from raw market-by-order (MBO) data — every add, cancel, modify, and fill, replayed in sequence to recover the book state at any instant. From the reconstructed book we compute microstructure features: top-of-book imbalance, depth-weighted imbalances, order-flow imbalance, and so on. Standard pipeline, hundreds of files, nothing exotic.

The bug

In MBO semantics, a Modify event can change an order's price, its size, or both. Our Book.modify() handled the size — and silently ignored the new price. Modified orders stayed parked at their old price level with stale size, forever.

How bad is that in practice? It depends on how often modifies move price. We measured it: 79% of modifies on ZN (10-year Treasury futures) change price. On ES (S&P 500), 89%. On GC (gold), 99%. This was not a corner case. It was the main case.

Why every check passed

Here is the part worth publishing. The pipeline had been audited and marked verified correct. The audit checked spread, mid-price, forward-return recomputation, event ordering (no look-ahead), and that the level-1 imbalance lay in [−1, 1]. Every check passed.

They passed because of a helper called best_uncrossed(), which walks past crossed price levels to find a sane top of book. Orders stranded at stale prices frequently crossed the book, and best_uncrossed() dutifully skipped over them — recovering the correct prices while leaving phantom size sitting at the top of the book. Every price-based quantity looked clean. Every size-based quantity was quietly wrong.

The range check deserves its own sentence: confirming a feature lies in [−1, 1] confirms it is well-formed, never that it is correct. A plausible-looking number is the most dangerous kind of wrong.

The measured damage on ZN: our stored level-1 imbalance correlated 0.52 with the true value and carried the wrong sign 19% of the time. Six features were corrupted — every size-based feature in the panel. Mid-price, spreads, and all forward returns were untouched, which is precisely why the damage was invisible to a price-oriented audit.

How it was actually caught

Not by staring harder at the code. It was caught by a results-blind reimplementation: a separate session reimplemented the MBO replay from scratch, without reading the original Book code, and compared outputs. One comparison lit up immediately: the independent reconstruction's raw book was crossed 0.00% of the time; the original's was crossed 92–100% of the time. A correctly maintained book should essentially never cross. Ours crossed almost always, and a masking helper had been hiding it.

An earlier version of our own handoff notes had even flagged the crossed-book artifacts — and dismissed them as "not a bug in this stage, don't chase them." That note was wrong, and it delayed the discovery. The artifact you explain away is the artifact that owns you.

The fix, and what verification means now

The one-line description of the fix is boring: Book.modify() now moves the order to its new price. The full panel was regenerated the same day (1,005 files, zero errors). The interesting part is the verification standard the bug forced on us: a second results-blind session, sampling ~150 random rows per symbol-day across six liquid symbols, comparing against its own independent reconstruction.

Producer and checker are now different implementations by construction. The original "verified correct" stamp had been issued by the same code path that produced the data — which is to say, it verified nothing.

Lessons

1. Audit the quantities your pipeline doesn't display. Our checks clustered around prices because prices are what you look at. The bug lived in sizes because nothing ever looked at them.

2. Well-formed is not correct. Bounds checks, schema checks, and NaN checks catch malformed data. They are silent on plausible garbage.

3. Independent reimplementation beats code review. The bug survived audits of the code that contained it. It did not survive one afternoon of comparison against a from-scratch replay written by someone who had never seen the original.

4. Chase your anomalies. The crossed book was visible long before the bug was found. It had been noticed, rationalized, and filed under "known artifact." Every subtle bug we have found announced itself early as an anomaly somebody explained away.
